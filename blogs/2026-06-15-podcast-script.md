# Episode: Why Most Moats Fail: The Validation Scope Problem

## COLD OPEN

Zillow's algorithm could predict home prices with 1.8% error. [pause] They spent years proving it. Millions of transactions. It became a household name.

Then they tried to use it to flip houses. [pause]

Two years later, the error rate was 7.2%. Same algorithm. Same data. Same team. [pause] An $880 million loss.

The moat they thought they had completely disappeared when the context shifted just slightly. From predicting the past to predicting the future.

Today we're going to talk about why that happens. And why the companies moving fastest right now, raising the most money, announcing the biggest deals, aren't necessarily the ones who will win.

---

## INTRODUCTION

I'm Prashant. This is Thinking in Strategies. Every week I take one business or strategy story and try to actually explain what's going on beneath the surface.

This week, validation scope. It's the gap between where a company proves their advantage and where they actually deploy it. And right now, in the middle of the largest consolidation wave since 2020, it's the difference between companies that dominate for a decade and companies that collapse spectacularly.

---

## ACT 1 — CONTEXT

So here's where we are.

Salesforce just bundled AI agents into enterprise contracts at $550 per user per month. That's a 175% price increase. Google signed a $920 million per month deal with SpaceX for AI compute capacity. Kissht, an Indian fintech lender, filed for IPO after growing revenue 150% year over year.

The narrative is always the same. These companies moved fast. They grabbed market share. They're winning.

But when you look at what actually happens to companies during consolidation, there's a different pattern. It's not about timing. It's about something nobody talks about.

Let me give you three examples.

Zillow. For years, their Zestimate algorithm was phenomenal at one thing. Predicting what a house is worth today. Historical data. Millions of transactions where the right answer was already known. 1.8% error rate. Exceptional.

In 2020, they tried something new. Instead of just estimating current prices, they started buying homes, fixing them up, and reselling them. The algorithm would predict future value after improvements.

It seemed logical. Same technology. Same market. Just a slightly different use case. [pause]

Two years later, $880 million loss. The algorithm's error rate on future predictions wasn't 1.8%. It was 7.2%. The moat evaporated.

Second example. Intel.

For 25 years, from 1980 to 2005, Intel's advantage was vertical integration. That means they designed chips and manufactured them in their own factories, called fabs. A single fab cost $5 billion to $10 billion to build. But it gave Intel total control over performance. Gross margins, that's the percentage of revenue you keep after paying to make the product, were 50 to 60%.

Then the world changed.

A Taiwanese company called TSMC got so good at manufacturing chips for other companies that it became better than owning your own fab. Apple, Nvidia, AMD designed their chips and handed the designs to TSMC. TSMC's scale meant they could build better chips, faster, cheaper.

Intel's moat became an anchor. [pause]

They kept investing in fabs. Kept insisting their approach was superior. By 2025, Intel's gross margin had collapsed to 32.7%, down from 56% in 2005. [pause] That's a 10 year margin death spiral.

The moat didn't just weaken. It became the thing killing them.

Third example. Microsoft.

In 2014, the world was still buying software licenses. You paid Microsoft once for Windows or Office, installed it, that was it. Microsoft's moat was bundling. Windows came on every PC. Office became the default. Network effects locked customers in.

But Satya Nadella, who became CEO in 2014, saw what was happening. People were switching to Google Docs because it was free. Startups were building on Amazon Web Services, not Windows Server. Mobile apps were bypassing Windows entirely.

The bundling moat was eroding. [pause]

Microsoft revalidated. They tested whether cloud subscriptions could be a better moat. Pay monthly. Software runs on Microsoft's servers. Access it from anywhere.

It worked. By fiscal year 2026, 85% of Microsoft's revenue came from cloud services. They didn't fight to preserve the old moat. They built a new one before the old one collapsed.

So that's the setup. Three companies. Zillow lost $880 million. Intel spent a decade collapsing. Microsoft became more valuable than ever. Same situation, consolidation and platform shift, totally different outcomes.

---

## ACT 2 — THE ARGUMENT

So what's the pattern here?

It's not timing. Zillow moved early into home flipping. Intel invested billions trying to stay ahead. Microsoft was actually late to cloud compared to Amazon.

The pattern is validation scope. [pause]

Validation scope is the gap between the conditions where a moat was proven and the conditions where it gets deployed.

Zillow proved their algorithm worked on historical data. Millions of transactions where the right answer was already known. They deployed it to predict future prices, where the right answer wouldn't be known for months and where their own buying activity would move the market.

The validation context, past and passive observer, didn't match the deployment context, future and active participant. The moat evaporated.

Intel validated vertical integration in a world where owning your own fab beat outsourcing. That world ended. TSMC got better. Intel never revalidated. They kept deploying a moat that was proven in conditions that no longer existed.

Microsoft revalidated early. They tested cloud subscriptions in actual customer pilots before going all in. The validation context matched the deployment context. The moat held.

[slower] Moats don't travel. A moat validated in one context doesn't automatically work in another.

Now here's the part that I think most people miss.

When you read about consolidation in tech right now, the story is always about who's moving fastest, who's extracting the most pricing power, who's signing the biggest deals. But that's not what predicts outcomes.

What predicts outcomes is whether the moat was validated in conditions that match where it will actually compete.

Let me show you how to measure that. There are four tests.

**Test one. Validation context match.**

Was the moat validated in conditions that match where it will compete?

Score this zero to 10. If the moat was proven in the exact environment it will face, score 8 to 10. If it was proven in similar conditions with some known differences, score 4 to 7. If it was proven in fundamentally different conditions, score zero to 3.

Zillow: validated on past prices, deployed to future prices. Zero match.

Intel: validated when vertical integration beat outsourcing, deployed when outsourcing beat vertical integration. Zero match.

Microsoft: revalidated cloud subscriptions in customer pilots before full deployment. Strong match.

**Test two. Moat strength in representative conditions.**

How strong is the advantage after accounting for transfer risk?

Zillow thought they had 1.8% error, but that was on past data. On future data, 7.2%. The real moat was near zero.

Intel thought vertical integration gave them a performance edge, but TSMC's outsourced chips were faster. The real moat was negative.

Score this zero to 10. If the moat delivers 40% or more cost or performance advantage in deployment conditions, score 8 to 10. If it delivers 15 to 40%, score 4 to 7. If it delivers under 15% or is untested, score zero to 3.

**Test three. Commoditization timeline.**

How fast will competitors close the gap?

If you have 18 to 24 months before the advantage disappears, you can extract pricing, build the next moat, and move on. If you have 4 to 6 years, you can relax. If you have 6 to 12 months, you're in a race.

Score this zero to 10. If commoditization takes 4 or more years, score 8 to 10. If 2 to 4 years, score 4 to 7. If under 2 years, score zero to 3.

**Test four. Next moat defensibility.**

Can you build a new moat before the current one erodes?

Microsoft had Azure, Office 365, and Xbox subscriptions all running in parallel. Intel had nothing. Zillow tried to pivot to home flipping but had no backup plan.

Score this zero to 10. If you have a validated next moat already generating revenue, score 8 to 10. If you have one in testing, score 4 to 7. If you have nothing, score zero to 3.

[pause]

Now let's apply this to what's happening right now. Three companies consolidating today. Google's AI infrastructure play. Kissht's India fintech IPO. Salesforce's Agentforce bundling.

**Google. 7.25 out of 10. Likely winner.**

Google's moat is custom AI chips called TPUs. They've been using them internally since 2015 to run YouTube recommendations, Google Search, Maps. The advantage is real. 44% lower total cost of ownership, that's the full cost of buying, running, and maintaining the system, compared to Nvidia GPUs.

Validation context match: 7 out of 10.

The moat was validated internally. Running Google's own models on Google's own infrastructure. Now they want to sell TPU access to external customers who will run their own models.

There's transfer risk. Google's models are optimized for TPUs. External customers' models might not be.

But here's the clever part. Google just signed a $920 million per month deal to provide compute to SpaceX's xAI. [pause] That's a hedge. If external customers struggle to get the same 44% advantage, Google still has a massive customer locked in.

The validation context isn't perfect, but they're testing the transfer and hedging the risk.

Moat strength: 8 out of 10. 44% cost advantage is exceptional. Even if external customers only see 25 to 30% advantage after transfer, that's enough to win.

Commoditization timeline: 6 out of 10. AI inference, that's running trained models millions of times per day, is commoditizing fast. Maybe 24 to 36 months before Nvidia, AMD, and others close the cost gap. But Google has a fallback. Foundation models. Gemini. If the chip moat erodes, the model moat takes over.

Next moat defensibility: 8 out of 10. Gemini is already in market. Google isn't dependent on TPUs alone.

Total: 7.25 out of 10. Google is likely to win because they validated in representative conditions, the moat is strong, and they have a backup plan.

**Kissht. 5.25 out of 10. Borderline.**

Kissht is an Indian fintech lender. They lend money to consumers without requiring collateral, called unsecured lending, mostly for purchases like phones or appliances.

Their moat is measurement discipline. They claim to predict who will repay loans better than competitors. The proof is their gross non performing asset ratio, that's the percentage of loans that go bad. Kissht's ratio is 2.12% as of Q4 fiscal year 2026. Industry average is around 2 to 3%. So they're average, not exceptional.

Validation context match: 6 out of 10.

Kissht validated on 5,000 plus borrowers in a protected market. Protected because India's Reserve Bank hasn't yet allowed tech giants like Google Pay or Paytm to offer direct lending.

But the RBI is expected to finalize lending rules in December 2026. [pause]

If Google Pay and Paytm enter lending, Kissht's competitive environment changes completely. Validation context was "us versus small lenders," deployment will be "us versus Google and Paytm with 500 million users each."

Moat strength: 5 out of 10. 2.12% non performing loans is fine, but it's not a structural advantage. If it were 1.5%, that would signal genuinely better credit models. At 2.12%, it's "we're competent," not "we're exceptional."

Commoditization timeline: 7 out of 10. If the RBI opens lending in December 2026, the window is 18 to 36 months before distribution, reach to customers, beats measurement, credit model quality.

Next moat defensibility: 4 out of 10. Kissht hasn't signaled what comes after credit measurement. Do they build a super app? Partner with a bank? The IPO filing doesn't say.

Total: 5.25 out of 10. Borderline. If the RBI delays lending liberalization, they have runway. If it happens on schedule, they're a likely acquisition target for a bank that wants the credit models but doesn't want to build them in house.

**Salesforce. 2.75 out of 10. Very high risk.**

Salesforce launched Agentforce. An AI agent product that automates customer service, sales workflows, marketing tasks. They bundled it into enterprise contracts at $550 per user per month. That's a 175% price increase over the previous product.

The pitch: AI will save you so much money on headcount that the price increase is worth it.

The problem. [pause] 56% of customers report return on investment below 5% after 6 to 18 months of use.

Return on investment, that's the financial benefit compared to the cost. Below 5% means the product is basically delivering nothing.

Validation context match: 2 out of 10.

Salesforce validated Agentforce internally. They claim it works. But they've published zero backward looking ROI data from real customer deployments. They publish forward looking ROI calculators, tools that estimate future savings, but those aren't validation. Validation is "here's what actually happened."

Silence on real data means the moat isn't validated in deployment conditions.

Moat strength: 2 out of 10. If 56% of customers see under 5% ROI, the product isn't delivering value. The moat is actually customer lock in from 3 year contracts signed in 2024 to 2026, not product quality.

Commoditization timeline: 4 out of 10. Awareness of alternatives like Claude, that's Anthropic's AI, plus Zapier, a no code workflow tool, is growing. By mid 2027, customers will realize they can build similar workflows for $50 to $100 per user instead of $550. The bundling advantage lasts 18 to 24 months, maybe less.

Next moat defensibility: 3 out of 10. Salesforce has Data Cloud, but it's not yet proven as a standalone moat. If Agentforce fails, do customers stay for Data Cloud? Unknown.

Total: 2.75 out of 10. Salesforce faces very high risk of a churn cliff, that's when customers cancel subscriptions all at once, in Q3 to Q4 2027 when three year contracts signed in 2024 to 2025 come up for renewal.

And this is where it gets genuinely interesting.

The Salesforce situation mirrors almost exactly what happened to Cisco in 1999. Cisco acquired a company called Cerent for $6.9 billion. Cisco's moat was routers and switches for networking. They'd validated that moat for years. Cerent made optical networking equipment, a slightly different market.

Cisco assumed the moat would transfer. Great at routers, therefore great at optics. [pause]

It didn't transfer. The optics market commoditized faster than expected. Cerent never recovered its acquisition price. Cisco had to write off the whole thing.

Salesforce is doing the same. Assuming that CRM, customer relationship management software, dominance transfers to AI agents without intermediate validation.

History says this fails.

## ACT 3 — COUNTERARGUMENT

Now, the pushback I'd expect here is this.

Salesforce has locked customers into 3 year contracts. Even if the product doesn't deliver value, those customers are stuck until 2027 or 2028. That gives Salesforce time to fix the product, improve ROI, and retain customers before the contracts renew.

And honestly, it's a fair point. [pause]

But here's why I still think the risk is real.

The contracts do buy time. But they also create a ticking clock. If Salesforce doesn't improve customer ROI from under 5% to at least 15 to 20% by Q1 2027, customers will have 18 to 24 months of data proving the product didn't deliver. And by mid 2027, awareness of alternatives will be mature.

The churn cliff happens when three things converge. Contracts renewing. Customers with hard data proving low ROI. And awareness of cheaper alternatives. All three will hit in Q3 to Q4 2027.

The lock in doesn't prevent the cliff. It just sets the date.

---

## TAKEAWAY

If you take three things from today:

One. [slower] Moats don't travel. A moat validated in one context doesn't automatically work in another. Zillow's algorithm was exceptional at predicting past prices, worthless at predicting future prices. Same tech, different context, $880 million loss.

Two. Consolidation winners aren't determined by who moves fastest or who extracts the most pricing power. They're determined by validation scope. Whether the moat was tested in conditions that match where it will actually compete. Google scores 7.25 out of 10 because they validated TPUs internally, hedged transfer risk with the SpaceX deal, and have Gemini as a backup. Salesforce scores 2.75 out of 10 because they never validated Agentforce in deployment conditions and 56% of customers report under 5% ROI.

Three. The companies extracting the most pricing power right now face the highest collapse risk. Salesforce bundled AI at a 175% price increase with zero backward looking ROI data. That's price extraction on an unvalidated moat. History says this ends badly. Intel refused to revalidate when the world changed, turned their moat into a 10 year anchor. Microsoft revalidated early, pivoted before bundling eroded, now 85% cloud revenue.

The pattern is clear. Speed doesn't predict outcomes. Validation scope does.
