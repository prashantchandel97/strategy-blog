# Episode: When Regulatory Moats Become Liabilities

## COLD OPEN

Accenture just lost $240 million in contracts. [pause]

Not because a competitor undercut them. Not because clients ran out of budget. Because they couldn't explain to federal regulators how they calculated their prices.

Meanwhile, Salesforce sold the exact same thing, AI automation saving client time, and had zero cancellations. [pause]

Same technology. Same value proposition. Completely different regulatory outcome.

Today we're going to talk about why regulatory moats last 5 to 10 years, not forever. And why the companies that won the last enforcement cycle are about to get hit by the next one.

---

## INTRODUCTION

I'm Prashant. This is Thinking in Strategies. Every week I take one business or strategy story and try to actually explain what's going on beneath the surface.

This week: regulatory moats. Specifically, why the thing that protects your business today becomes the thing that kills it tomorrow when enforcement priorities shift. We're watching this happen in real time right now. June 2026 was the inflection point, and most companies haven't noticed yet.

---

## ACT 1 — CONTEXT

So here's where we are.

For the past three years, 2024 through 2026, regulators have been focused on one thing: pricing transparency. The FTC made algorithmic pricing, that's when software sets prices dynamically based on data the customer can't see, a top enforcement priority. The EU AI Act, which went into full effect in August 2026, requires explainability for any automated system that affects what people pay.

The concern was simple. Companies were using algorithms to hide margin extraction. You buy a plane ticket, the price changes based on how desperate the algorithm thinks you are. You subscribe to software, the pricing adjusts based on signals you never see. Regulators said: if customers can't understand why they're paying what they're paying, that's deceptive.

At the same time, enterprise software was moving toward outcome-based pricing. Instead of charging per user or per hour, companies started charging based on results. Time saved, revenue generated, customer satisfaction improvement. By early 2026, about 22% of enterprise software contracts were fully outcome-based. Another 60% were hybrid models mixing seat licenses with outcome payments.

The logic made sense. CFOs want to pay for results, not activity. If your AI saves the sales team 20 hours a week, why not charge based on the hours saved instead of the number of software licenses?

But here's the problem. To charge based on time saved, you need to prove causality. You need to show that your software caused the savings, not something else. Maybe the employee just got better at their job. Maybe the workload decreased. Maybe the process changed for unrelated reasons.

That requires a counterfactual. What would have happened without your software? And counterfactuals are, by definition, unmeasurable. You can estimate them, you can model them, but you cannot audit them.

And if you can't audit the price calculation, you have regulatory liability under the new transparency rules. [pause]

That's the environment Accenture and Salesforce were operating in when June 2026 hit.

---

## ACT 2 — THE ARGUMENT

Let me walk you through what happened to Accenture, because it's the clearest example of a regulatory moat collapsing.

On June 18, 2026, Accenture told investors they were cutting growth guidance from 5% down to 3 to 4%. That doesn't sound dramatic until you do the math. That's over $240 million in contracts that just evaporated.

The reason: outcome-based pricing became undefendable under FTC scrutiny.

Accenture's entire consulting model is outcome-based. You hire them to solve a problem. Improve margins, launch a product, restructure operations. The value is in the result. You can't break consulting work down into countable micro-actions the way software can.

When clients realized they'd have to defend outcome-based contracts to regulators who now view outcome measurement as algorithmic pricing, they walked away. Not because the work wasn't valuable. Because the regulatory risk was uninsurable. [pause]

Now contrast that with Salesforce.

Salesforce introduced something called Flex Credits. Instead of charging based on deals closed faster or time saved, which are outcomes you can't audit, they charge 10 cents per action. An automated email sent, a customer support case resolved, a data enrichment performed.

These are all auditable events. You can count them. You can verify them in a log file. There's no counterfactual required. The FTC can look at your bill, see 10,000 actions at 10 cents each, and confirm the math. No algorithmic pricing risk. [pause]

Salesforce had zero contract cancellations from the same regulatory pressure that cost Accenture $240 million.

Same AI automation. Same promise of saving client time. Completely different regulatory exposure.

This is the first key insight. [slower] Regulatory moats require you to solve the regulator's problem, not just the customer's problem.

Accenture optimized for demonstrating ROI to clients. Salesforce optimized for passing regulatory scrutiny. In 2026, the second one mattered more.

So that's what's happening. Here's why it matters.

The companies that built moats by solving the 2024 to 2026 enforcement priority, transparency and explainability, now have a window. Maybe five years, maybe ten. That's how long regulatory moats last before enforcement priorities shift.

And we can see the shift starting right now.

Let me show you the pattern with a second company: Razorpay.

Razorpay processes 30 to 40% of India's digital payments. They're a payment aggregator. That means they sit between merchants and banks, handling the complexity of connecting to multiple payment methods. Credit cards, UPI, digital wallets, net banking. The merchant integrates once with Razorpay, and Razorpay handles the rest.

In September 2025, the Reserve Bank of India published final directions for payment aggregators. The big requirement: 25 crore rupees in net worth. That's about $3 million US. Not revenue. Capital you have to have sitting in the company, unencumbered, to prove you can survive operational shocks and settlement failures.

For Razorpay, already at scale, this was manageable. They raised capital, met the requirement, got their license.

For startups trying to enter the market, it's a wall. You need to raise $3 million just to get the license before you process a single transaction. And because payment aggregation is a volume business with thin margins, you earn 1 to 2% per transaction, you need massive scale to justify that upfront capital.

The result: Razorpay has a regulatory moat. Not because they have better technology, though they might. Because they met a compliance requirement that prevents new competitors from entering unless they have venture backing or a deep-pocketed parent company.

This is exactly what killed a company called INFLUISH. They built a creator payment settlement layer. 500,000 creators, 50% month-over-month growth. The moat was real. Once creators earned money across multiple platforms through INFLUISH's system, switching costs were high. You'd have to reconfigure payouts for every brand deal, every platform, every contract.

But INFLUISH didn't have 25 crore rupees. They couldn't raise it fast enough. So they got acquihired. The settlement layer became a feature inside someone else's product, not a standalone business. The regulatory requirement killed the company even though the product worked and customers wanted it. [pause]

Now here's the part that I think most people miss.

Razorpay's moat is durable for five to ten years. That's how long it takes for scaled competitors, think PhonePe, Paytm, Google Pay, to decide the payment aggregation market is worth entering, apply for their own licenses, and spend the 18 to 24 months navigating regulatory approval.

Once they enter, Razorpay's moat erodes to normal competition on fees, integration quality, customer service. The regulatory barrier kept out startups. It doesn't keep out giants.

This is the second insight. [slower] Regulatory moats are time-limited defenses, not permanent business moats. They give you a window to build something else, distribution, brand, switching costs, that survives after the regulatory barrier falls or competitors clear it.

And this is where it gets genuinely interesting, because the enforcement cycle is shifting right now.

June 2026 saw three major antitrust events.

One. Google's Chrome and Android divestiture order was enforced. This came out of the 2023 DOJ antitrust case. Google has to spin out Chrome and license Android on fair terms to competitors. The remedy is structural. Break up the company's control over distribution.

Two. Meta lost a jury verdict in an antitrust case over Instagram's acquisition of a competitor. The finding: Meta used its dominance to prevent competitive threats.

Three. Uber lost an antitrust case over driver classification and market power in the gig economy.

These are not regulatory enforcement like the FTC algorithmic pricing crackdown. These are antitrust cases targeting market concentration and exclusionary conduct. That's when a dominant company uses its position to block competitors or lock in customers unfairly.

The shift is from what I'll call Cycle 1, that's 2024 to 2026, focused on deception, transparency, algorithmic pricing, to Cycle 2, 2027 to 2028, focused on market concentration, bundling, and exclusionary conduct. [pause]

Let me make this concrete.

In Cycle 1, Salesforce won by being transparent. Flex Credits pass FTC scrutiny because you can audit every charge.

In Cycle 2, Salesforce's dominance becomes the risk.

They own roughly 23% of the global CRM market. Customer relationship management software, the system companies use to track sales, customer data, support tickets. Now they're bundling something called Agentforce, their AI agent platform, into CRM subscriptions. It costs $125 to $650 per user per month depending on the tier. If you're a CRM customer, in most packaging tiers you get Agentforce whether you want it or not.

That's bundling. And bundling by a dominant player to extend market power into a new category, in this case AI agents, is exactly what antitrust enforcers target.

The precedent is Microsoft bundling Internet Explorer into Windows in the 1990s. Microsoft wasn't punished for having a dominant operating system. They were punished for using OS dominance to kill competition in browsers by making Internet Explorer the default and free.

Salesforce bundling Agentforce into CRM is the same structure. Using CRM dominance, where they have lock-in through customer data and workflows, to win in AI agents, where they don't yet have dominance. [pause]

Razorpay faces similar risk.

Processing 30 to 40% of India's digital payments creates market concentration. If the Competition Commission of India investigates and finds exclusionary conduct, for example favorable terms to merchants who use only Razorpay, or blocking access to certain payment rails for competitors, that 30 to 40% market share becomes liability.

The regulatory moat, the payment aggregator license, that kept out startups becomes evidence of dominance in an antitrust case.

This is the third insight, and the hardest one. [slower] Regulatory moats built in one enforcement cycle become liabilities in the next cycle.

What protected you from Cycle 1 enforcement, transparency, licensing, exposes you to Cycle 2 enforcement, dominance, bundling.

## ACT 3 — COUNTERARGUMENT

Now, the pushback I'd expect here is this: if regulatory moats only last five to ten years, why do we see companies like Visa, Mastercard, and major banks maintaining regulatory advantages for decades?

And honestly, it's a fair point. Payment networks have had regulatory moats for 50 years. Banks have had capital requirement moats since banking regulation began.

Here's why I still think the pattern holds.

Those companies didn't rely on regulatory moats alone. Visa and Mastercard built network effects. The more merchants accept Visa, the more valuable it is to cardholders. The more cardholders use Visa, the more merchants want to accept it. That's a product moat that exists independent of regulation.

Banks built switching cost moats. Moving your checking account, your direct deposit, your auto-pay bills, your entire financial life to a new bank is painful enough that most people don't do it even when a better option exists.

The companies that survive multiple enforcement cycles are the ones that layer regulatory defenses on top of non-regulatory moats. The regulatory moat buys you time. Five to ten years. You use that time to build something else that survives when the regulatory environment shifts.

The companies that fail are the ones that treat the regulatory moat as the entire strategy. INFLUISH had product engagement but no regulatory defense, they couldn't meet capital requirements. Accenture had client relationships but no regulatory defense and no product lock-in, clients can switch consultants relatively easily.

[pause]

So the counterargument actually strengthens the main point. Regulatory moats work, but only as part of a layered defense.

---

## TAKEAWAY

If you take three things from today:

One. Regulatory moats are real, but they're time-limited. You get five to ten years before enforcement priorities shift and the thing that protected you becomes the thing that exposes you. Accenture learned this with outcome-based pricing. Salesforce and Razorpay are about to learn it with market concentration.

Two. The enforcement cycle is shifting right now from Cycle 1, transparency and deception, to Cycle 2, antitrust and market dominance. June 2026 was the signal: Google divestiture, Meta and Uber antitrust losses all in the same month. If your moat is market share or bundling, prepare for scrutiny.

Three. The companies that survive layer defenses. Regulatory compliance to pass current enforcement. Preparation for next-cycle enforcement, can you unbundle without breaking the business? And non-regulatory product moats, switching costs, data lock-in, distribution advantages, that survive regardless of which regulations apply. One layer isn't enough.
