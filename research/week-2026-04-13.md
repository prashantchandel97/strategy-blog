## Monday–Friday — R (Researcher) — Catch-up: The Great Agent War: Who Becomes the Enterprise AI Operating System?

[Note: Catch-up entry covering full week April 13–18, 2026 — pipeline was being rebuilt. No entries written earlier this week.]

This week crystallized a battle that has been building for 18 months: the race to become the **orchestration layer** for autonomous AI agents in the enterprise. Three players made significant moves, and the outcome may determine the most important distribution question in tech since the app stores of 2008.

### The Setup: Why "Who Orchestrates the Agents" Is the Only Question That Matters

Enterprise software used to be a seat-count business. You licensed Microsoft Office or Salesforce CRM by the head, and every employee you hired was another dollar of ARR. Agentic AI breaks this model at the foundation. If a single AI agent can handle the work of 10 knowledge workers — and the early evidence suggests the top frontier models are approaching this in narrow domains — then the historical correlation between headcount growth and software spend collapses.

This creates a crisis for every incumbent SaaS vendor. But it also creates a massive prize: whoever becomes the **workflow orchestrator** — the platform through which agents are deployed, governed, audited, and connected to data — captures a fee on every AI action taken in the enterprise, regardless of headcount. This is a fundamentally more scalable and durable revenue model than seat licensing.

Two companies this week made explicit bids for that position. A third arrived as an uninvited disruption.

### Salesforce: The Front Office Bet

Salesforce's Agentforce — powered by the Atlas Reasoning Engine, incubated at Salesforce AI Research — is the company's most consequential product bet in a decade. Unlike early generative AI implementations that relied on static prompts handed to a foundation model, Atlas uses iterative planning: agents autonomously search for data, evaluate potential actions against company policies, refine their plans in real-time, and only then execute.

The early performance numbers are striking. Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff claimed "90 to 95 percent resolution on all service and sales issues" in early Atlas deployments. Third-party benchmarks from pilot customers show a 2x increase in response relevance and a 33% improvement in end-to-end accuracy in customer service applications versus competitors. Whether these hold at enterprise scale is the open question — but the directionality is real.

The commercial model Salesforce has chosen is revealing: Flex Credits, where 100,000 credits cost $500, with each "action" consuming credits based on complexity. This is a pure consumption model — the same logic that made AWS the most powerful economic engine in tech history. Salesforce is not selling you seats anymore. It is selling you compute-equivalent units of enterprise cognition.

Strategically, Salesforce is positioning Agentforce as the "front office" AI OS — customer experience, sales, service, marketing. Its data moat (decades of CRM data across 150,000+ enterprise customers) is the proprietary context that makes Atlas's planning loop more accurate than a generic model. The thesis: an agent trained on your company's historical deal patterns and customer interaction data will dramatically outperform one that is not, and Salesforce is the only company with that data at scale.

### ServiceNow: The Back Office Control Tower

ServiceNow's bet is structurally different and, in some ways, more ambitious. The Santa Clara company has spent the last decade building what CEO Bill McDermott now calls the "AI Control Tower" — a cross-enterprise workflow and governance layer that sits above applications. The logic: AI agents need to operate across systems (ERP, CRM, ITSM, HRMS), and whoever governs the cross-system orchestration layer owns the value.

The numbers behind this thesis are substantial. ServiceNow has set a target of $1 billion in AI-specific ACV (annual contract value) by end of 2026, against a total subscription revenue forecast of $15.53–$15.57 billion (20% YoY growth). The company's "Assist Packs" — a new consumption-based model mirroring Salesforce's Flex Credits logic — price AI by the task rather than the seat. In Q3 2025, ServiceNow reported a 55x increase in AI Agent Assist consumption over five months, with 4x quarter-over-quarter growth in AI control tower traffic.

An April 14 deep-dive analysis from FinancialContent highlighted the key tension: ServiceNow is arguably the only company that already sits in the cross-system governance position at the Fortune 500. Most large enterprises already use ServiceNow to manage IT workflows, HR processes, and increasingly supply chain — it is the connective tissue nobody sees. This is a powerful position for orchestrating agents. But it also faces a structural challenge: as AI agents automate the work that generates ServiceNow's "workflow events," volume could shrink even as each unit becomes more valuable. Goldman Sachs and Stifel are bullish; a UBS downgrade on April 10 — calling AI a structural headwind rather than tailwind for ServiceNow's seat-based model — rattled the stock and opened the debate wider.

ServiceNow is also quietly moving toward challenging SAP and Oracle in ERP. If successful, "AI Control Tower" becomes literal: the system running your supply chain, finance, and HR, plus governing every AI agent that touches those systems. That is not a SaaS company anymore. That is an enterprise OS.

### The Wildcard: OpenAI's Codex Goes Desktop-Native

On April 16, 2026, OpenAI shipped a major Codex desktop update that most enterprise software investors underweighted but should not have. The update ships three capabilities in one: full **computer use** (Codex can now operate any Mac application — clicking, typing, taking screenshots — running multiple parallel agents without interfering with the user's active work), a **90+ plugin marketplace** combining app integrations and MCP servers, and persistent **memory and automation** (agents that schedule their own future work and resume tasks across days or weeks).

The strategic significance is the distribution model. Unlike Salesforce (which requires enterprise implementation) or ServiceNow (which requires an IT team and a multi-year contract), OpenAI's Codex reaches the individual user directly — at the desktop layer, before IT governance can intercept it. This is the classic "bottoms-up SaaS" motion, except the unit of adoption is not a Slack workspace or a Figma file — it is an autonomous agent running on your computer.

The security community noticed immediately. A Help Net Security analysis published April 17 flagged the key question: "Codex can now operate between apps. Where are the boundaries?" The concern is not malware — it is the governance gap. An enterprise that deploys Salesforce Agentforce has audit trails, policy enforcement, and IT visibility. An employee who downloads Codex and grants it computer use has none of that. If OpenAI captures end-user adoption before IT governance catches up, it could lock out both Salesforce and ServiceNow from the AI action layer — not because it built a better orchestration platform, but because it won distribution first.

This is the "app store moment for AI agents" question from the topics backlog, now arriving in real form. The question is not which platform has the best agent technology. It is which platform achieves the distribution lock-in that makes switching costs prohibitive — and whether that lock-in comes top-down (enterprise contract → IT governance → mandated tooling) or bottom-up (individual adoption → viral spread → IT eventually rubber-stamps what everyone is already using).

### The Structural Pattern

What is actually happening is familiar if you zoom out: three different companies are trying to capture the same strategic layer (agent orchestration) using three different distribution wedges.

Salesforce is using **data moat + front-office relationships**. ServiceNow is using **cross-system governance + back-office integration depth**. OpenAI is using **consumer-grade UX + direct desktop access**.

History suggests these battles do not end with one winner — they end with the enterprise splitting into two or three "stacks" that each control a domain, with an interoperability layer in the middle that becomes a new rent-collecting position. (See: AWS vs Azure vs GCP, which did not consolidate but did create a multi-cloud management layer as the new prize.) The open question is whether the orchestration layer above AI agents is a winner-take-all market or a oligopoly — and which companies are positioned on the right side of that answer.

What is clear from this week: the race is now explicit, the commercial models are crystallizing around consumption rather than seats, and the incumbent enterprise software vendors (SAP, Oracle, Workday) have not yet made their decisive move. That silence is itself a data point worth examining.

## Friday — A (Analyst) — The Middleware War Nobody's Calling By Its Right Name

The Researcher did what good researchers do: found the most dramatic frame for this week's moves and ran with it. Salesforce, ServiceNow, and OpenAI racing to become "the enterprise AI OS" is a compelling headline. It's also, I'd argue, precisely the wrong historical analogy — and getting the analogy wrong leads to the wrong strategic conclusions.

Let me start with what the Researcher got right, because the foundation is solid. The pricing shift from seats to consumption is real and structural. Salesforce's Flex Credits at $500 per 100,000 credits ($0.10 per action) have been verified. ServiceNow's Now Assist ACV trajectory toward $1 billion by end of 2026 is confirmed — the product has more than doubled year-over-year and is the fastest-growing launch in the company's history. OpenAI's April 16 Codex update with full computer use, 90+ plugins, and persistent memory is exactly what it was reported to be. The facts check out. The interpretation needs stress-testing.

### The Wrong Parallel

The Researcher gestures at "AWS vs Azure vs GCP" as the structural analog — a market that didn't consolidate into one winner but created multi-cloud management as a new rent-collecting layer. That's closer, but the better parallel isn't cloud. It's the enterprise middleware wars of the 2000s.

Here's why this matters: TIBCO, WebMethods, webMethods, BEA, and eventually MuleSoft all competed to become the "integration layer" — the orchestration bus that connected every enterprise application. The pitch was identical to what Salesforce and ServiceNow are making today. Whoever routes the data between applications captures a toll on every enterprise workflow. The outcome? No single vendor won. The market fragmented into domain oligopolies (TIBCO owned financial services real-time data; MuleSoft owned API-led integrations in Salesforce shops; IBM owned the mainframe-adjacent middleware). And then Salesforce acquired MuleSoft for $6.5 billion in 2018 — not because MuleSoft was winning the middleware war, but because Salesforce needed the integration layer to make its own platform stickier. That acquisition was an admission that you cannot win the orchestration layer from the application layer alone.

Now look at what's happening. ServiceNow is trying to win the orchestration layer from the ITSM/workflow application layer. Salesforce is trying to win it from the CRM layer. OpenAI is trying to win it from the desktop. All three are making the same structural error that every middleware aspirant has made: assuming that application-layer distribution translates into orchestration-layer capture.

### The Steelman Counter-Argument

Here is the strongest version of the counter-case — the one I find most dangerous for the bull thesis on Salesforce and ServiceNow specifically. This is not really a platform war. These companies are bolting AI onto architectures designed for a pre-agentic world, and the market knows it.

SAP's own data is damning on this point: only 3% of SAP customers use Joule in production, and 77% of AI-active enterprises use non-SAP solutions. That's not because Joule is bad — it's because enterprise AI adoption is hitting the data quality wall first. Gartner estimates that through 2026, organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects due to insufficient data quality. If agents fail not because the orchestration platform is wrong but because the underlying data is siloed and dirty, then winning the orchestration layer is winning a race to a destination nobody can reach. The "platform war" framing overstates how ready enterprises actually are for autonomous agentic workflows.

### Where I Land: The System of Record Is the Moat

Here is the thesis I think the blog should be built around, because it's both contrarian and analytically defensible: **the enterprise AI orchestration battle will not be won by the company that builds the best agent runtime. It will be won by the company that owns the system of record that agents act upon.**

This reframes the entire competitive landscape. Salesforce's Agentforce is powerful inside Salesforce CRM data. ServiceNow's Control Tower is powerful inside ITSM and IT workflow data. OpenAI's Codex is powerful at the desktop task layer. But none of them own the deepest data moat in the enterprise — transactional history. That belongs to SAP and Oracle.

The Researcher flagged "strategic silence" from SAP and Oracle. This is incorrect. SAP Joule Studio went generally available in Q1 2026. SAP's Cash Management Agent is in production, reducing time spent on manual cash positioning by up to 80% through autonomous bank statement analysis. Oracle launched 22 Fusion Agentic Applications on March 24, 2026 — embedded directly in Oracle Fusion Cloud, spanning finance, HR, supply chain, and CX. Oracle's explicit framing is that being "native to the transactional system" is the decisive differentiator over copilots and orchestration layers that sit above the data. That is not silence. That is the most coherent strategic argument in this entire competitive landscape — and it's coming from the two companies nobody in the CRM/ITSM world is taking seriously enough.

The Siebel-to-Salesforce transition is instructive here, but not in the way the Researcher implied. Salesforce won not because it built a better orchestration layer but because it found a distribution wedge (SMBs, bottoms-up, no IT approval required) that bypassed Siebel's enterprise strongholds. OpenAI's Codex is attempting exactly this playbook against Salesforce and ServiceNow — bottoms-up desktop adoption before IT governance catches up. But the Siebel parallel cuts the other way too: Siebel's real downfall was that Salesforce's data model was simpler and more accessible, not that Siebel's data was inferior. SAP and Oracle have the opposite problem. Their data is the richest in the enterprise. Their access model is what's broken. If either company cracks the agent-native UX problem — which Oracle's Fusion Agentic Applications are explicitly targeting — the CRM and ITSM vendors will find themselves in Siebel's position: rich relationships, legacy architecture, and a new entrant that made the same capability feel effortless.

The consumption model (Flex Credits, Assist Packs) is table stakes — every major vendor is converging on it. The governance gap created by Codex is real but temporary; IT departments close governance gaps, and the regulated enterprises that Salesforce and ServiceNow serve will not tolerate unaudited desktop agents for long. The durable strategic question is simpler: when an AI agent needs to cancel an order, approve a headcount request, or reconcile a supplier invoice, which system does it write back to? That system's vendor wins.

**Key framework:** Systems of Record as Moat — the strategic concept that durable competitive advantage in workflow software flows from ownership of authoritative transactional data, not from the layer that reads or routes it. First articulated by Marc Andreessen's "software is eating the world" era analysis; now the decisive variable in the agentic transition.

**Strongest counter-argument:** The "system of record" framing assumes that SAP and Oracle can ship agent UX fast enough to matter. Their track record on product velocity is poor — SAP Joule has 40+ agents and 2,400 skills, yet only 3% of customers use it in production. Speed of distribution may defeat depth of data moat, particularly if OpenAI's bottoms-up Codex strategy locks in end-user habits before IT mandates a governed platform. The blog needs to hold both of these tensions.
