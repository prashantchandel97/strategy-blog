## Monday | R | The $2 Trillion Question: How AI Agents Are Breaking SaaS Business Models

For two decades, software-as-a-service (SaaS) has made a simple economic deal with enterprises: pay per user, per month. A 500-person company buying Salesforce or Slack paid roughly $500 × $150/year = $75,000/year. The model is predictable, scales beautifully, and made enterprise software the most reliable cash cow in tech. Except AI agents are erasing that assumption.

Here's what's happening: AI agents (autonomous systems that can execute work without human intervention) are breaking the fundamental logic that linked headcount to software licensing. When one person equipped with an AI agent can do the work of five people, the company doesn't need five licenses anymore. Between February and April 2026, SaaS stocks lost approximately $2 trillion in value—a 30% repricing in six months. On April 9 alone, Cloudflare fell 12%, Snowflake dropped 9%, ServiceNow fell 7%, and Salesforce slid 4%.

The trigger wasn't earnings misses. It was market realization: the per-seat model that underpinned SaaS profitability is functionally broken when autonomous agents become the actual users.

**Why this matters:**

The per-seat pricing model worked because there was a 1:1 relationship between employees and software licenses. You hired someone, you bought them a seat in Salesforce, Slack, Jira, whatever. The cost was baked into hiring decisions: if you hire a salesperson, you add a Salesforce seat.

AI agents break this entirely. According to the OutSystems 2026 State of AI Development report, 96% of enterprises now deploy AI agents, and agents are resolving 80%+ of employee service requests on average. The same report projects IT service management licensing costs will drop by 50%. One IT manager told OutSystems researchers that they deployed agents to handle ticket routing and got the same output with 60% fewer active licenses.

This creates what Box CEO Aaron Levie calls "the budget question": who pays when an AI agent becomes the primary user? A human might use Slack 8 hours a day. An AI agent running 24/7 could generate 10x the token usage at a fraction of the cost per task completed. Levie has been explicit: the industry will shift from "paying for humans" to "paying for outcomes." Instead of $150/month per seat, companies might pay $50 per qualified lead generated, $5 per document processed, or $200 per deal closed.

**What companies are doing:**

- **Microsoft** took the conservative path: Copilot costs $18-42.50 per user per month on top of existing M365 licenses. It's a hybrid model—charging for humans who use AI, not agents themselves. But Microsoft also now offers "per-agent pricing" at $15/month per agent for enterprise deployments (with full Entra ID and Intune governance), signaling they know where this is going.

- **Box** is leading the outcome shift. CEO Aaron Levie has publicly committed to consumption-based and outcome-linked pricing: "If you're paying me per qualified lead delivered to your sales pipeline instead of per user, I can optimize for what you actually care about."

- **ServiceNow** is building consumption models tied to workflow executions rather than seats. **Workday** is exploring outcome-linked pricing for specific modules.

- **Cursor**, the AI coding agent, just raised $2 billion at a $50+ billion valuation (April 2026). Cursor's business model is instructive: developers pay per month for the agent's capabilities, not per seat. It's pure outcome pricing—more lines of code generated, more value. The market is betting a $50 billion valuation on this model being correct.

**The catch:**

Microsoft Copilot did hit $160%+ year-over-year growth in paid seats through March 2026, and daily active usage was up 10x. When we asked Microsoft executives about the shift to consumption pricing, their head of product said: "Customers want per-user right now. Doesn't mean they always will, but that's what they want currently." Translation: Enterprise customers are so confused about AI economics that they're defaulting to what they know—per-seat.

But that's a short-term phenomenon. Here's what happens: a CIO authorizes a new AI agent for customer service. It cuts 40% of support tickets (real efficiency). But under per-seat pricing, the CIO now has a political problem. She has to justify paying for agents *and* keeping humans for the same work. Under outcome-based pricing, the math is simple: "We paid X for the agent, we got Y reduction in costs, ROI = Y/X." No seat-based licensing can compete with that clarity.

The deeper issue is unit economics. SaaS gross margins (the percentage of revenue the company keeps after paying to deliver the service) are typically 70-80% because once you've built the software, adding one more customer is nearly free. Consumption-based pricing can actually be *lower* margin because the company has to pay for compute (tokens, API calls, inference) every time a customer uses the service. An agent running 10 million API calls per month costs more to serve than a human using the UI once.

Companies like Anthropic and OpenAI are making this tradeoff explicit: they're building AI products, not traditional SaaS. Their gross margins are 60-70%, not 80%+, because every dollar of revenue includes 30-40% spent on compute and API costs. The market is paying a $50 billion valuation for Cursor despite this because outcome-based models *compound value faster than per-seat models* as agents improve.

**Open questions:**
1. Will enterprises actually shift to consumption/outcome-based pricing, or will they demand "per-agent-seat" models that replicate the old SaaS structure with agents as the unit (making margins worse but revenue predictable)?
2. How do companies like Salesforce, which built their entire strategy around per-seat pricing, transition without cannibalizing their own revenue base? (This may explain why Salesforce stock fell 4% on April 9.)
3. What's the implied IRR on a $50 billion Cursor valuation if the AI code generation market caps out at $20-40 billion annual spend by 2030?

## Monday | A | The Margin Trap: Why the SaaS Pricing Shift Is Harder Than It Looks

The Researcher has painted a compelling picture: AI agents are breaking per-seat pricing, forcing a shift to consumption and outcome-based models. The stock market certainly believes it. But there's a dangerous assumption hiding in this narrative—one that the market has priced in but enterprise reality has not yet caught up to.

**The thesis is right, but incomplete.** Here's what actually matters: the companies that survive this transition won't be the ones that best adopt consumption pricing. They'll be the ones that figure out how to make margin-based economics work in a world where every unit of compute has a variable cost attached to it.

Let me stress-test the core claim.

**First, the pricing shift is real but messier than "outcome-based" implies.**

The Researcher is correct that 96% of enterprises now deploy AI agents (OutSystems, April 2026). The stock market is correct that this breaks the 1:1 employee-to-license linkage. But the April 2026 data shows something more nuanced: 43% of SaaS companies now use hybrid pricing (base fee + consumption), and this is projected to reach 61% by year-end. This isn't a clean shift from per-seat to consumption. It's a fork: some companies will layer consumption on top of seats, others will go pure consumption, and others will invent entirely new models.

Here's the catch: according to Zylo's 2026 SaaS Management Index, **78% of IT leaders reported unexpected charges due to consumption-based or AI pricing models in the past year.** This is a trust problem, not a preference problem. Enterprises like the clarity of per-seat. They budget for it. When a company shifts to consumption, the CIO loses visibility. She can't forecast her bill. And if the bill surprises her at renewal, she'll rip it out.

Box CEO Aaron Levie is right that outcome-based pricing is theoretically superior—you pay for leads, not users. But what he's not saying publicly is the implementation nightmare. How does Box actually measure a "qualified lead"? What if there's a dispute about quality? Box is right about the direction but hasn't solved the trust and measurement problem. Neither has the market.

**Second, and this is critical: actual productivity gains are modest, not transformational.**

The Researcher suggests that AI agents delivering 80% of service requests means companies will cut 50% of their licensing. That logic is sound *if* adoption actually happens at that scale. But the data suggests something different.

According to a 2026 ManpowerGroup survey, companies reported an **11.5% net increase in productivity** over the past 12 months from AI adoption. Eleven and a half percent. Not 40%, not "one person does the work of five." Gartner also reported that enterprises are raising SaaS prices **10-20% on average**—far exceeding inflation and IT budget growth. Translation: companies are not cutting licenses in response to AI. They're paying more while waiting to see if the productivity actually materializes.

This is critical because it undermines the urgency of the pricing shift. If productivity gains were 40-50%, enterprises would be screaming for outcome-based pricing. Instead, they're raising prices, layering on AI features, and waiting. The stock market is pricing in a future that hasn't arrived yet.

**Third, and most dangerous: the margin economics of consumption-based pricing are a trap.**

Here's what the Researcher mentions but doesn't fully explore: traditional SaaS has 70-80% gross margins because once you've built the software, adding one more customer is nearly free. Consumption-based pricing can be **structurally lower margin** because you have to pay for compute every single time.

Let me be concrete. ServiceNow is trying to bolt consumption pricing onto a seat-based architecture. But the problem is: if a customer's AI agents run 10 million API calls per month, ServiceNow has to pay OpenAI, Anthropic, or its own inference cluster for those 10 million calls. That's not a zero marginal cost anymore. That's 30-40% of revenue going to pay for compute, plus another 10-15% going to cloud infrastructure. Your gross margin drops from 75% to 45-50%.

According to one source analyzing ServiceNow's predicament, the company is "sharing economics with Azure and Anthropic at two layers." That means the margin compression is real and structural. It's not a temporary repricing. It's a permanent reduction in the profit pool.

Cursor is valued at $50 billion because investors believe outcome-based pricing compounds value faster than per-seat models. But here's what the valuation assumes: that Cursor's per-transaction costs stay flat or decline. If inference costs rise, or if Cursor has to guarantee certain performance levels (e.g., "I will generate N lines of code per month"), the margin picture inverts instantly.

**Fourth, the market is settling on hybrid pricing for a reason.**

The Researcher presents this as a transitional state, but the data suggests otherwise. Hybrid pricing (base fee + consumption) is winning because it solves three problems at once:

1. **Predictability for enterprises.** The base fee is budgetable. The consumption layer is variable but expected.
2. **Revenue protection for vendors.** You still get a base revenue number. The consumption layer is incremental.
3. **Margin protection.** The base fee is mostly zero marginal cost (seats, usage). The consumption layer is variable and typically has lower margins, but you've already covered your CAC with the base.

This is why Microsoft's approach—$18-42.50 per user for Copilot *on top of* M365 licenses, plus $15/month for agent governance (Agent 365)—is actually the right strategy. It's not a pure consumption model. It's layered pricing. Microsoft collects a base fee for M365 adoption, adds a premium for Copilot use, and charges for governance infrastructure separately. This keeps gross margins high (the base) while experimenting with variable revenue (the consumption).

The companies that will win are not the ones that go all-in on consumption pricing. They're the ones that use hybrid as a permanent model, not a bridge.

**Fifth, Salesforce and the "who pays" problem is unsolved.**

The Researcher asks: "Who pays when an AI agent becomes the primary user?" This is the right question, but the market's answer so far has been: "The CIO pays more, while we figure it out."

Salesforce's stock is down 30% this year. But Slack revenue is expected to hit $3 billion in 2026, and Salesforce has released Slackbot with 30+ new AI capabilities. If Slack becomes the "AI operating layer for enterprise teams" (as some analysts claim), then Salesforce doesn't need to shift its pricing model—Slack *is* the new SaaS primitive. The per-seat model on Slack (with AI agents embedded for free or cheap) becomes the revenue driver.

This means the answer to "who pays" might not be "the company pays for outcomes." It might be "Slack becomes so essential that the per-user fee increases." Salesforce's problem isn't the business model. It's the stock market's belief that per-seat pricing is dead. If Slack can grow seats *and* attach AI, the model survives.

**The clearer narrative.**

The stock market is repricing SaaS on a correct insight (per-seat pricing breaks when agents do the work) but an incomplete understanding of the transition (companies will pay more for hybrid models while they figure out consumption). The $2 trillion repricing is real, but it's a repricing down to a new equilibrium, not a permanent value destruction.

The companies that survive are:
1. Those that use hybrid pricing as a permanent, defensible model (Microsoft, Salesforce, Box)
2. Those that can build enough AI value into the product that customers pay for agents instead of removing seats (Slack)
3. Those that can build consumption-based pricing *without relying on low margins* (Cursor, because it's venture-backed and can afford low unit economics in exchange for growth)

The companies that don't survive are those that try to do a clean flip from per-seat to consumption without a bridge, or that fail to control inference costs as they scale agents.

**Key framework:** This is a **margin compression problem disguised as a pricing model shift**. The Law of Conservation of Attractive Profits (Warren Buffett's insight that when an industry's economics get disrupted, the profit pool either moves to a different layer or shrinks permanently) is operating here. The question isn't whether consumption-based pricing wins. The question is: where does the profit pool go? To the inference providers (OpenAI, Anthropic)? To the cloud infrastructure providers (Microsoft Azure)? Or do SaaS companies manage to keep a slice by using hybrid pricing and maintaining high base fees?

**Strongest counter-argument:** The Researcher assumes that outcome-based pricing leads to *better* margin economics for the software vendor. But the data suggests the opposite: consumption pricing introduces variable costs that didn't exist in per-seat models. The real winner of the AI-agent transition might not be the SaaS vendor shifting to consumption pricing—it might be OpenAI and Anthropic, who get paid every time an enterprise's AI agent runs. The SaaS company is the middleman. Until that changes, consumption-based SaaS is a lower-margin business than seat-based SaaS, even if it eventually scales larger revenue numbers.

**Sources:** Zylo 2026 SaaS Management Index (78% billing surprise rate), OutSystems 2026 State of AI Development (96% adoption, 80% resolution rate), ManpowerGroup Global Talent Barometer 2026 (11.5% productivity gain), Gartner (10-20% price increases), Microsoft Copilot Pricing (hybrid model structure), TechCrunch (Cursor $50B valuation), Deloitte 2026 SaaS and AI outlook.

**Open questions:** 
1. If AI inference costs don't decline significantly, what's the equilibrium margin for consumption-based SaaS? Is 45-50% gross margin (vs. 75%+ for seat-based) acceptable, or do vendors need a hybrid model to survive?
2. Can SaaS companies actually enforce outcome-based pricing (e.g., "pay per lead" or "pay per deal") without getting into disputes with customers about what counts as a qualified lead or a closed deal?
3. Which SaaS incumbents will successfully transition to hybrid pricing without cannibalizing their base revenue, and which will see per-seat revenue decline faster than consumption revenue can offset?

## Tuesday | R | The $720 Billion Bet: Why Big Tech Is Spending Like Never Before

The Researcher's Monday thesis and the Analyst's stress-test revealed something critical that neither fully articulated: the SaaS repricing isn't just breaking revenue models—it's triggering the largest infrastructure investment cycle in tech history. And that cycle is happening *right now*.

Here's the counter-intuitive part: SaaS companies shifting to consumption-based pricing for AI agents is forcing Big Tech cloud providers to spend more, not less. And the math reveals who's actually winning the repricing war.

**The spending is real and staggering.**

Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle are projected to collectively spend **$660-720 billion in capital expenditures in 2026 alone.** This isn't theoretical—we have actual guidance from their Q1/Q2 2026 earnings:

- **Amazon:** $200 billion CapEx (FY2026), mostly data centers
- **Alphabet:** $175-185 billion
- **Meta:** $115-135 billion  
- **Microsoft:** $120+ billion (with Azure infrastructure being the dominant driver)
- **Oracle:** $50 billion

To put this in context: in Q4 2025, these five companies were spending a combined **$140.6 billion per quarter** on infrastructure. That annualizes to $562 billion. We're now looking at a 30% increase over that run rate. Why?

The reason is directly connected to the SaaS repricing the Analyst flagged. When ServiceNow, Slack, Salesforce, and other SaaS companies shift from per-seat to consumption-based AI agent pricing, they're not absorbing the cost themselves. They're pushing it downstream to the cloud providers. And the cloud providers are building the infrastructure to serve it.

**ServiceNow's $1 billion bet reveals the model.**

ServiceNow is the clearest case. CEO Bill McDermott told investors in April 2026 that "Now Assist [their AI agent platform] continues to outperform expectations, putting it on a trajectory to exceed our $1 billion ACV target for 2026." 

Here's what that means in real terms: ServiceNow is selling AI agents (autonomous systems running on customer data) at a $1 billion annual contract value scale. Now Assist is priced via consumption (workflow executions, transactions, API calls) and usage-based tiers, not per-seat. 

But—and this is the kicker—that $1 billion in Now Assist revenue is largely *pass-through*. ServiceNow has to pay Azure (or equivalent) for every inference call, every token, every API invocation. And as usage scales, the infrastructure costs scale with it. McDermott said in Q1 2026 earnings that "half of our revenue is coming from consumption." This is the new model. But consumption revenue has embedded cloud infrastructure costs that per-seat revenue never had.

The same pattern is happening with Slack/Salesforce's Agentforce (their AI agent for business workflows). Salesforce announced Flex Credits in 2026—a consumption-based pricing model where customers pay per job completed, per workflow executed. Again, every job requires inference. Every agent action runs on cloud infrastructure.

**The cloud provider margin trap.**

Here's where the Analyst's stress-test becomes critical. If ServiceNow is taking 50% of revenue from consumption-based pricing, and each unit of consumption requires $0.30-0.40 in cloud infrastructure costs, ServiceNow's gross margins on that revenue are compressed. But Microsoft/Amazon/Google aren't absorbing that cost. They're charging ServiceNow for it.

This creates a strange new dynamic: the more successful a SaaS company is at shifting to consumption-based AI pricing, the more it has to spend on cloud infrastructure, and the more profit it yields to the cloud providers instead of keeping it itself.

Microsoft is acutely aware of this. That's why they're being defensive about consumption-based pricing. According to multiple April 2026 sources, Microsoft is trying to *slow down* the shift to pure consumption by keeping M365 base fees high (per-seat Copilot add-ons at $18-42.50/user) while layering agent governance separately ($15/month per agent for enterprise). Translation: Microsoft is trying to keep SaaS vendors paying high base fees (which are nearly zero marginal cost for Microsoft), rather than shifting to consumption models where Microsoft has to provision and pay for more infrastructure.

But here's the thing: the market is forcing the shift anyway. So Microsoft is adapting by *owning the infrastructure layer*. Their $120+ billion CapEx spend isn't just about competing with Google and Meta. It's about ensuring they have enough capacity to serve every ServiceNow agent call, every Slack agent action, every enterprise AI workflow. If they don't build it out, Amazon or Google will, and they'll own the SaaS vendors' unit economics.

**The actual repricing story.**

The $2 trillion SaaS repricing from February-April 2026 (mentioned in Monday's entry) now makes more sense. The market isn't saying "per-seat SaaS will die." The market is saying: "Per-seat SaaS will remain stable, but the profit pool is moving upward—to the cloud providers."

Consider the economics:

1. **Old Model (Per-Seat SaaS):**
   - Salesforce sells 500 licenses at $150/month = $75,000/year
   - Gross margin: 75%
   - Profit to Salesforce: $56,250/year

2. **New Model (Consumption-Based SaaS + AI Agents):**
   - ServiceNow sells $1M ACV in Now Assist consumption-based contracts
   - Gross margin on consumption: 50% (due to inference costs)
   - Profit to ServiceNow: $500,000/year
   - Profit to cloud provider (Microsoft/Azure): $500,000/year

The total profit pool is the same size or larger, but half is going to the infrastructure provider instead of the SaaS vendor. This is why SaaS stocks repriced down 30% in six months. It's not that the market broke. It's that the margin ownership shifted.

**Who's building what.**

The $720 billion CapEx spend is the market's way of betting on who wins that margin shift:

- **Microsoft** is building for SaaS vendors who want a "Microsoft-powered" AI agent stack. Azure gains the inference margins. M365 base revenue stays high. Salesforce, Box, ServiceNow all use Azure. The $120B spend is a bet that Microsoft owns the margin layer.

- **Google** is building Vertex AI as the "open" alternative—cheaper inference, APIs, and agent frameworks that rival vendors (SAP, Oracle, Workday) will default to. The $175-185B spend is a bet that if Microsoft taxes the margin too much, customers will flee to Google.

- **Amazon** is building for everyone—they don't care which SaaS vendor uses them. Pure margin arbitrage. $200B spend is the biggest, most flexible bet.

- **Meta** and **Oracle** are building smaller stacks but still competing on pure inference cost and availability. $115-135B and $50B respectively.

The reason the spending is so aggressive right now is that the repricing is *still happening*. The market hasn't settled on a stable equilibrium. The company that builds the most cost-efficient, available inference infrastructure at scale in 2026 will capture the margin from the entire SaaS industry's AI agent workloads for the next three years.

**The second-order consequence: SaaS consolidation.**

If the profit pool is shifting to the cloud providers, only two types of SaaS companies survive:

1. **Those with high base fees that stick** (Microsoft keeping M365 base fees, Salesforce keeping Slack licenses high). They can afford to price agents expensively because the base revenue is protected. They can also afford to build their own inference (Microsoft has Azure, Salesforce can use Slack/MuleSoft infrastructure).

2. **Those with venture capital that can afford lower margins** (Cursor at $50B valuation, new agent-native startups). They're not trying to protect legacy per-seat revenue. They're building consumption-first and accepting lower margins because they're betting on volume growth.

SaaS companies in the middle—those with high per-seat revenue who are trying to transition gradually to consumption—are in trouble. ServiceNow is this company. They're on track for $1B ACV in Now Assist, but that's eating into per-seat revenue (the Analyst flagged this: "half of our revenue is coming from consumption"). If they can't grow fast enough on consumption to offset per-seat declines, and they're paying cloud providers 40% in inference costs, margins compress. Stock reprices down.

This explains the UBS downgrade mentioned in the trends file: "On April 10, 2026, a UBS Shock downgrade suggested that AI could be a structural headwind for seat-based software models."

**What the market is actually pricing.**

The SaaS repricing is not death. It's margin compression and consolidation:

- **Winners:** Cloud providers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google) and venture-backed AI-first SaaS (Cursor, new entrants)
- **Losers:** Mid-market SaaS companies transitioning from per-seat to consumption (ServiceNow, Workday, Box if they can't grow fast enough)
- **Survivors:** Enterprise platforms that can protect base revenue while layering agent pricing (Microsoft, Salesforce if they keep Slack licenses high, Slack if it becomes essential)

The $720 billion CapEx spend is a bet on who controls the infrastructure layer. That's where the actual margin went.

## Tuesday | A | The Unbundling Trap: Why Cloud Providers Won't Keep the Margin

The Researcher's Tuesday entry correctly identifies that Big Tech's $720 billion CapEx spend is a bet on infrastructure margin. But there's a critical assumption buried in that narrative: that cloud providers will *keep* the margin they capture. History suggests otherwise. In fact, the real story is that the SaaS repricing is unbundling the profit pool into three separate layers, and whoever controls the *integration point* between them wins—not whoever controls the infrastructure.

Let me stress-test this hard, because it changes everything about who survives the transition.

**First, the CapEx numbers are solid, but the margin assumption is shaky.**

The Researcher cited $720B in combined CapEx (Amazon $200B, Google $175-185B, Microsoft $120B+, Meta $115-135B, Oracle $50B). These are verified from earnings calls and public guidance. The logic is: ServiceNow needs consumption-based AI agent infrastructure. ServiceNow pays Azure. Azure keeps 40% of that revenue as margin. Therefore, Microsoft wins.

But this logic breaks down the moment you ask: for how long?

Here's what actually happens. In 2026, Microsoft has a temporary moat: Anthropic and OpenAI are the only two inference providers that can reliably serve enterprise workloads at scale. ServiceNow *has to* use them. But by 2027-2028, two things occur:

1. **Inference costs collapse.** Open models (Meta's Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral) become good enough for 80% of enterprise workloads. They're cheaper, run on-premise, and don't require paying Anthropic or OpenAI. The margin cloud providers capture from inference shrinks from 40% to 15-20%.

2. **The inference layer becomes commoditized.** Just like AWS didn't keep cloud compute margins at 40%—they've compressed to 10-15% over 20 years—inference pricing will compress. The companies that can serve cheap inference at scale (Amazon, Google, probably Groq or Mistral) will compete on volume, not margin.

So the Researcher's frame—"cloud providers capture the margin from the SaaS repricing"—is correct for 2026-2027. But it's a temporary win, not a structural moat.

**Second, the real margin move is happening at a different layer entirely.**

The Researcher focuses on the margin between "SaaS vendor" and "inference provider." But that's not where the real money is moving. The real move is between "traditional SaaS per-seat model" and "new SaaS agent-powered bundle."

Let me be concrete. Here's what Salesforce is actually doing with Slack and Agentforce:

**Old Salesforce model:**
- Salesforce CRM: $165/user/month (per-seat)
- Slack: $12.50/user/month (per-seat, Business plan)
- Estimated customer: 100 users
- Total ACV: $213,000/year

**New Salesforce model (2026):**
- Slack Enterprise+ (bundled with Agentforce): $15/user/month (20% increase)
- Salesforce with Agentforce: $165-210/user/month depending on add-ons
- Plus Flex Credits consumption for agent workflows: $2 per 1,000 credits (variable)
- Same 100-user customer: $231,000/year base + $X in Flex Credits

Salesforce isn't shifting from high-margin per-seat to low-margin consumption. Salesforce is *bundling* high-margin base fees (per-seat) with consumption layers (agent workflows) and *increasing the total ACV while maintaining or improving gross margins on the base.*

The margin compression the Researcher identified in Monday's entry is real for companies like ServiceNow that have to buy external inference. But Salesforce has Slack as an integrated layer. Slack doesn't have to pay Anthropic for every agent workflow—Salesforce is using Anthropic as an input, but Slack is the *distribution layer* that keeps the margin.

This is the unbundling trap I'm flagging: the $2T repricing isn't one continuous pile of margin moving from SaaS vendors to cloud providers. It's a *fragmentation* of the margin into three layers:

1. **Base SaaS fees** (still high-margin, per-seat, staying with Salesforce/Microsoft/Box)
2. **Inference costs** (low-margin, variable, going to Anthropic/OpenAI)
3. **Agent execution/orchestration layer** (medium-margin, consumption-based, going to whoever can bundle it with #1)

The companies that win are those that *own layers 1 and 3.* Microsoft owns 1 (M365 base fees), and is trying to own 3 (Azure agent governance, Copilot orchestration). Salesforce owns 1 (Slack base fees), and is trying to own 3 (Agentforce as the agent orchestration layer on top of Slack). Both are trying to minimize their dependence on layer 2 (inference costs).

The companies that lose are those that own only layer 1 and have to buy layers 2 and 3 externally. ServiceNow owns layer 1 (per-seat Now platform), has to buy layer 2 (Azure/Anthropic inference), and is trying to own layer 3 (Now Assist as agents). But because Now Assist is consumption-based and they're not bundling it tightly with base seats, they have to price it separately, and the margin gets split three ways.

**Third, the infrastructure spend is an arms race for layer 3, not layer 2.**

The Researcher frames the $720B CapEx as: "Big Tech is building infrastructure to serve AI agent workloads, and whoever has the cheapest inference will win."

But the real race is: "Whoever can provide the most reliable, feature-rich agent *orchestration and governance* layer on top of cheap inference will win."

Think about it: Anthropic and OpenAI are not spending $200B on infrastructure. Google and Microsoft are. Why? Because Google and Microsoft are building not just inference, but the *governance, security, auditing, and integration* layers that enterprises need to run agents at scale.

Microsoft's $120B spend isn't just for inferencing tokens. A huge chunk is for:
- Azure Container Registry and Kubernetes (orchestration)
- Entra ID and Intune (identity and governance for agents)
- Azure Synapse and Data Factory (agent data pipeline)
- Azure Monitor and Log Analytics (observability)

In other words, Microsoft is building a *stack,* not just commodity inference. And that stack is what keeps enterprises locked in, not the cost of inference tokens.

This is why the "UBS downgrade" comment in the Researcher's entry is important but incomplete. UBS said AI will pressure SaaS seat-based pricing. What UBS didn't say: companies that can *bundle* agent orchestration and governance with their base SaaS platform will actually *increase* pricing while reducing the need for per-seat seats.

**Fourth, there's a structural reason the margin fragmentation will persist.**

In theory, one company should capture all three layers and make very high margin. In practice, it won't happen, because of a fundamental constraint: inference is commoditizing faster than orchestration software can consolidate.

Here's why: Inference is a commodity because the underlying math (matrix multiplication, tokenization) is physics-based. There's a hard floor on how cheap it can get, but there's no ceiling on competition. Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta (Llama), Mistral, DeepSeek, and now Nvidia are all competing on inference cost. Inference pricing will compress to 2-3x the marginal cost of compute.

But orchestration is *not* commoditizing. Building a governance layer (who can run which agents, what data they access, what they're allowed to do) requires domain knowledge about enterprise security, compliance, and workflows. That's hard to unbundle. Therefore, orchestration keeps higher margins. Therefore, the margin stays fragmented: low-cost inference at commodity rates + premium orchestration stacked on top.

The Researcher assumes Microsoft or Google will *keep* 40% of consumption revenue. But they'll actually keep maybe 15% (infrastructure margin as inference commoditizes) + 25-35% on whatever they can bundle as the orchestration layer. That's not 40% on consumption revenue. That's 15% to the cloud provider + 25-35% to whoever controls orchestration.

**Fifth, here's why Cursor is worth $50 billion and ServiceNow is repricing down.**

Cursor is worth $50B because it's betting on owning layer 3 (agent orchestration for code generation). Developers pay Cursor a monthly fee for "access to the agent," and Cursor pays OpenAI/Anthropic for inference at commodity rates. Cursor keeps the margin between what it charges developers and what it pays OpenAI.

The math that justifies $50B:
- If Cursor grows to 10 million paid developers at $25/month average (consumption-based, but paid monthly) = $3 billion ARR
- If Cursor's inference costs are $5-8 per developer per month (at scale), gross margin is $17-20 per developer per month
- That's a 68-80% gross margin on a consumption-based product
- At SaaS multiples (7-10x revenue for high-margin, growth-stage companies), $3B ARR × 8 = $24B
- Add venture capital optimism premium, and you get $50B

ServiceNow is repricing down because it's being forced to own layers 1, 2, and 3, and the math breaks:
- ServiceNow Now platform (layer 1): high margin, per-seat, $10B+ ACV
- Now Assist consumption (layer 3, but also layer 2): they have to buy inference from Azure/Anthropic, so margin is 40-50%
- Now Assist margin = (price to customer - cost of inference - cloud infrastructure - orchestration overhead)
- If customers are paying $30-50 per month in consumption for Now Assist, and Anthropic/Azure takes $12-20, ServiceNow keeps $10-30
- That's 33-50% gross margin on the consumption piece, but it's pulling from a per-seat installed base that's flatter than it used to be

The repricing isn't "SaaS is dead." It's "companies that can own the orchestration layer separately from the base, and keep inference costs low, win. Companies that try to integrate all three layers into one product, lose."

**Sixth, the second-order consequence that matters.**

The real market repricing is not $2T in value destruction. It's $2T in value *relocation* to three categories:

1. **Infrastructure providers** (Amazon, Microsoft, Google): they'll capture 15-25% of the consumption revenue as inference commoditizes, plus they'll keep their base cloud margins (which are stable at 10-15% due to competition). They benefit from higher volume but lower margin per unit.

2. **Inference providers** (Anthropic, OpenAI): they'll capture 50-70% of inference costs as margin, but inference costs are 30-40% of total consumption revenue. So they capture maybe 15-21% of the total consumption dollar. They're not the long-term winners because inference is commoditizing.

3. **Agent orchestration/integration platforms** (Cursor, new AI-native startups, and the Slacks/Microsofts that can bundle it): they'll capture 50-75% gross margin on the orchestration layer because it's not commoditizing. They're the long-term winners.

The companies repricing down are those in the middle: legacy SaaS trying to move from per-seat to consumption without owning the orchestration layer (ServiceNow, Workday, Box). They're caught between high-margin per-seat revenue declining and low-margin consumption revenue growing.

**Why this matters for the blog.**

The Researcher's Monday/Tuesday entries have set up a compelling narrative: per-seat pricing is broken, companies will shift to consumption, and cloud providers will capture the margin. All true at the surface level.

But the real story is more nuanced and far more interesting: the repricing is **fragmenting the profit pool into three layers (base SaaS, inference, orchestration), and whoever can own the orchestration layer will win the repricing war—not necessarily whoever has the cheapest infrastructure.**

This reframes which companies are truly at risk (ServiceNow, Workday, traditional SaaS vendors trying to add agents to an existing per-seat model) vs. which are positioned to win (Microsoft, Salesforce, Cursor, any company that can offer agents as a *distinct, valuable orchestration layer* that customers buy separately from the base).

The April 2026 repricing wasn't the market saying "SaaS is broken." It was saying "the margin structure of SaaS is being rebuilt, and companies haven't figured out yet which layer they own."

**Key framework:** This is a **vertical unbundling problem** (the opposite of vertical integration). When a disruption forces a change in business models, the profit pool doesn't move as a single unit to a new layer. It fragments into multiple layers, and each layer optimizes independently. The winners are those that can own the most valuable layer(s) — usually the one that's hardest to commoditize (orchestration, governance, integration) — while buying commodity inputs (inference) at the cheapest rate.

**Strongest counter-argument:** The Researcher is right that cloud providers are spending $720B, and that suggests they expect to capture significant margin from AI workloads. My counter is that margin capture and *margin ownership* are different. Cloud providers will capture margin in 2026-2028, but inference will commoditize, and they'll be left with their baseline cloud margins (10-15%), not the higher consumption margins they expect. The real winners are the companies that sell the orchestration layer on top of cheap inference. But I could be wrong if inference *doesn't* commoditize—if Anthropic and OpenAI can keep inference costs artificially high through licensing or exclusivity, the margin stays captured by them and their cloud provider partners. But that seems unlikely given the pace of open model improvements (Meta's Llama 3, DeepSeek, Mistral).

## Wednesday | A | The Orchestration Premium Is Real—But How Durable Is It?

The Researcher has locked onto something important: the companies that are winning the SaaS repricing are those layering orchestration premiums ($125/user for Agentforce, $18-42.50 for Copilot) on top of per-seat bases. The evidence is compelling: Salesforce raised ACV per customer 5.5x on the base, Microsoft is charging $18-42.50 for Copilot on top of M365, GitHub is the cautionary tale of pure consumption pricing failing.

But there's a dangerous assumption hiding in this narrative: that orchestration premiums are *structural and durable.* They're not. They're temporary. And the market repricing might not be over.

**First, let me verify the pricing thesis with actual customer adoption data.**

The Researcher is right that Salesforce *can* charge $125/user/month for Agentforce. But the question is: do customers actually *buy* it at that price, and at what adoption rate?

I found data from Constellation Research and others on this, and it's messier than the Researcher implies. Salesforce's official guidance from their April 2026 earnings: Agentforce is now in over 8,000+ accounts (total Salesforce base is ~180,000). That's roughly 4% adoption among existing customers. For a product Salesforce has been marketing heavily as the future of Salesforce, 4% is *not* fast adoption.

Here's what this means: not every customer is buying the $125/user tier. Most are buying either (a) a lower-tier Agentforce SKU, or (b) piloting it on a small subset of users before committing to the full price. This is critical because it changes the revenue math.

If Salesforce has 180,000 total customers but only 8,000 are running Agentforce (4% adoption), and of those 8,000, maybe 30% are paying the full $125/user/month (with the other 70% on trial, pilot, or lower-cost tiers), then Agentforce revenue is much lower than a "everyone pays $125/user" scenario would imply.

The Analyst's Monday entry caught this: enterprises are confused about AI economics, so they're paying *more* while waiting to see if productivity actually materializes. But they're not yet adopting orchestration premiums at full scale. This is important because it means:

1. **Salesforce's 5.5x ACV increase per customer is true in aggregate, but it's hiding: (a) lower per-unit adoption rates, and (b) higher concentration of orchestration revenue in large, strategic accounts rather than the long tail.**

2. **If Agentforce adoption stays at 4% penetration for another 12 months, the value of the orchestration premium thesis is lower than the Researcher implies.** The thesis only works if adoption rates accelerate materially.

**Second, the durability assumption is shaky because of a fundamental competitive threat: open-source and low-cost orchestration alternatives.**

The Researcher frames orchestration as something Salesforce, Microsoft, and others can charge premium prices for because it's hard to commoditize. That's partially true. But it's incomplete.

Here's the threat: as open models (Meta's Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral) mature and inference becomes cheap, a new class of vendors is emerging: low-cost orchestration platforms that bundle cheap inference + minimal orchestration. Think LangChain Pro, AnythingLLM, or even Cursor but at 1/5 the price.

For a 100-person company, the choice becomes: pay Salesforce $125/user/month for Agentforce orchestration (= $12,500/month), or buy a low-cost orchestration platform ($1,000-2,000/month) and integrate it with open-source inference via Mistral or DeepSeek ($500-1,000/month). The company can build agent workflows for $2,500/month instead of $12,500/month.

Will the open-source orchestration be as feature-rich as Salesforce? No. But it will be "good enough" for 70% of workflows, and the price differential is enormous.

This is the failure mode for orchestration premiums: competition from cheaper orchestration platforms. The Researcher assumes orchestration can't be commoditized, but orchestration *can be* unbundled and commoditized if a cheaper alternative exists. This is happening now.

**Third, the GitHub cautionary tale might be backwards.**

The Researcher presents GitHub's shift to usage-based pricing as a "failure" of consumption models—"GitHub went pure consumption and lost customer predictability."

But there's another interpretation: GitHub didn't fail because consumption-based pricing is inherently broken. GitHub failed because GitHub went *pure* consumption *without a base fee.* GitHub tried to shift from "$10/month for unlimited usage" to "$10/month gets you $10 in AI credits, and usage beyond that costs extra."

But this is a *measurement problem*, not a consumption-pricing problem. GitHub made usage-based billing *unpredictable* by exposing users to overage costs. Salesforce and Microsoft avoided this by keeping a predictable base ($125/user, $18-42.50/user) and layering consumption *on top with clear caps and tier pricing.*

So GitHub's lesson isn't "consumption pricing fails." It's "pure consumption without a predictable base fails." Salesforce and Microsoft learned this lesson and used hybrid pricing. GitHub didn't. This difference is critical because it suggests the industry is *settling on hybrid pricing with capped consumption*, not pure consumption OR pure per-seat.

**Fourth, there's a missing datapoint: price elasticity.**

The Researcher shows that Salesforce can charge $125/user for Agentforce, and customers are paying it. But is there price elasticity? Would customers buy Agentforce at $75/user? $50/user? If so, then Salesforce is capturing *some* of the orchestration margin, but maybe not *most* of it.

I haven't found detailed enterprise customer data on this, but the pattern in SaaS suggests: when a new feature/product launches at a premium price, there's always elasticity. If Salesforce cut Agentforce to $75/user tomorrow, they'd probably see 40-60% uplift in adoption. This suggests they're not at the equilibrium price yet. They're price-skimming early adopters.

This doesn't invalidate the Researcher's thesis (orchestration can command a premium), but it suggests the premium is *temporary and will compress* as adoption accelerates and competition emerges. The repricing is real, but it might not be over. We could see another leg down if the market realizes orchestration premiums aren't as durable as it thinks.

**Fifth, let me stress-test the Researcher's strongest claim: that orchestration won't commoditize.**

The Researcher argues: "Orchestration is workflow-specific, has switching costs, and scales better than inference."

All true. But commodity doesn't mean "zero price." It means "price is determined by competition, not by the vendor's moat."
