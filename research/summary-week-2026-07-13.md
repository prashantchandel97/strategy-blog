## Core Thesis
Meta's $145B AI capex strategy has three interlocking parts: custom silicon (MTIA Iris) to cut infrastructure costs 40–50%, cloud pricing arbitrage to monetize excess capacity, and fintech distribution in emerging markets. But execution is constrained at each layer. Iris is locked into TSMC through 2028, giving Meta no alternative if TSMC raises prices or delays capacity. Llama's slower-than-expected development (per Zuckerberg July 2) undermines the claim that price-based cloud competition can win enterprise share. Antitrust rulings forbid WhatsApp-Meta data-sharing, destroying the cross-leverage that makes fintech distribution valuable. Meta is effectively hedging three uncertain bets with a single $145B capex bucket. Only one or two likely win.

## The Key Tension
Meta owns unassailable *cost* advantages—custom silicon cuts per-inference cost 40–50%, enabling cloud pricing 20–30% below AWS/Azure while covering marginal cost. But cost advantage does not equal profit. In cloud: Llama development is "slower than expected" (Zuckerberg), so price alone cannot win enterprise customers; selling cheap compute to OpenAI/Anthropic accelerates the commoditization that destroys cloud margin. In fintech: India's antitrust ruling explicitly forbids WhatsApp-Meta data-sharing, blocking the cross-leverage that justifies the $900M CRED investment; PhonePe's 46% UPI dominance + $9.5B IPO suggest the competitive window is closing; CRED's exclusive model (credit scores >750) cannot scale to 500M WhatsApp users without destroying the trust positioning. In manufacturing: TSMC's CoWoS (advanced packaging) is sold out through end of 2026; Meta's Iris production is queued; Fab2's distributed small-fab vision could theoretically break TSMC's moat in 5+ years, but Intel Foundry (actual fabs, actual capex, 18A roadmap) is a more credible near-term alternative. Meta's real outcome: cost-optimization for internal ad ranking, not profit expansion for cloud or fintech.

## Open Questions for the Blog
1. **Is Meta's $145B capex one unified strategy or three separate hedges?** The capital allocation splits across infrastructure (AI), product (fintech), and manufacturing (custom silicon). If only one bet wins (e.g., custom silicon reduces Meta's own cost), does the $145B spend validate, or does it become a cost-cutting exercise masked as growth strategy?
2. **Which manufacturing constraint bites first: TSMC capacity (through 2028) or Fab2/Intel maturation (2027-2030)?** Meta's Iris production is locked into TSMC. If TSMC raises prices or extends delivery timelines, Meta's custom-silicon moat is delayed. If Intel Foundry 18A ships at acceptable yields by Q4 2027, Meta has leverage. If Fab2 never reaches production quality, TSMC's moat survives. The week's narrative hinges on which timeline is right.
3. **Can regulatory constraints be overcome, or do they cement the limits of Meta's emerging-market bets?** The antitrust ruling blocking WhatsApp-Meta data-sharing is the linchpin for fintech ROI. If India's regulators tighten enforcement, the CRED investment pays off only through internal regulatory moat, not distribution scale. If Meta's lobbying softens the ruling, fintech becomes viable. This determines whether Shah's appointment is genius or a $900M mistake.
