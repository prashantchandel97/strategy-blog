## Monday | R | The SaaS Pricing Model Is Cracking Under Agentic AI

The three biggest stories converging this week all point to the same crack in the foundation of enterprise software: the traditional per-seat pricing model—where customers pay a fixed amount per user per month—no longer makes sense when one AI agent can do the work of five humans.

**The headline evidence:**

Sierra, Bret Taylor's enterprise AI agent platform, just raised $950 million at a $15.8 billion valuation (May 4). That's real, serious capital flowing into a company whose entire premise is replacing human work with AI. But here's the uncomfortable part: Cloudflare, which reported record Q1 2026 revenue, announced the same day it was laying off 1,100 employees—20% of its workforce—not because business is bad, but because AI has made that many jobs obsolete.

CEO Matthew Prince didn't mince words. In a memo to staff, he said Cloudflare had seen "600% growth in internal AI usage" and that the company needed to "reimagine every team and function" for an "agentic AI era." He emphasized the cuts weren't about cost control—they were about structural necessity. The company is not cutting engineering or sales; it's cutting the support, administrative, and operational roles that AI can now handle. As Prince put it: "Very few engineers or customer-facing sales people impacted by our layoff. And we'll continue to hire like crazy in those roles."

This matters far beyond Cloudflare. Cloudflare is an infrastructure company—the kind of business that has traditionally benefited from SaaS economics: recurring revenue, easy scaling, high margins. If even Cloudflare has to permanently shrink its workforce *while hitting record revenue*, what does that say about every other SaaS company relying on headcount growth to justify their per-seat pricing?

**The math breaking down:**

The pricing problem is simple: SaaS has sold software on a per-user basis because each additional user meant additional value to the customer. One more account manager on your team? Buy one more Salesforce seat. Hire five more customer support reps? Buy five more Zendesk seats. The vendor captures value tied directly to how many humans the customer employs. Margins expand because the vendor's costs stay flat—a tenth user costs the same to serve as the first user.

But if a customer can deploy an AI agent that does the work of five support reps, they buy the same number of seats—or fewer. The per-seat model stops working. Some vendors are trying to redefine "seat" to mean "AI agent" instead of "human," but that's just accounting gymnastics.

The data backing this up is now unavoidable. According to Monetizely's pricing research and Gartner projections cited this week, some industries are seeing 30–40% seat reductions in early waves of AI deployment. Suno, the AI music generation startup now valued at $5 billion (raising >$250 million in its Series D), has 100 million users and just $300 million in annual recurring revenue. That's astounding—it suggests the path to profitability is through *output pricing* (pay per track generated) or *subscription tiers for power users*, not per-user licensing.

**Why this matters for enterprise software broadly:**

This week's funding announcements show capital is flowing away from the traditional SaaS model and toward agentic AI companies. Sierra's $15.8B valuation isn't coming because investors think Bret Taylor and Clay Bavor will build the next Salesforce clone. It's because they think Sierra is the platform that captures value *as customers shift from paying for human workers to paying for AI agents*.

The second-order effect: existing SaaS giants (Salesforce, Servicenow, Adobe, Atlassian) are under pressure to either:
1. Build their own agent layers and cannibalize their per-seat revenue
2. Shift pricing to consumption or outcome models (which often means *lower* revenue per customer)
3. Watch new competitors like Sierra take their best customers

The companies that have the flexibility to retool fastest—like Cloudflare, which just proved it can cut 20% headcount and keep growing—may survive. The ones still defending per-seat SaaS in 2027 will be at a disadvantage.

## Monday | A | The Pricing Model Isn't Cracking—It's Evolving, and the Winners Are Getting Richer

The Researcher's argument is elegant and feels right, but it misses a crucial distinction: **the per-seat model isn't breaking because customers are abandoning Salesforce and Servicenow for Sierra. It's breaking because the math inside existing seats is changing.** That's a fundamentally different story, with different winners.

Let me stress-test the main claims with data that complicates the narrative.

**First, the evidence that looks like the Researcher's thesis:**

Yes, SaaS gross margins are compressing. According to SFAI Labs and SaaS CFO reports from Q1 2026, companies shipping AI features are now reporting 60–70% gross margins instead of the traditional 75–82%. That's a 10–15 percentage-point hit. And yes, Cloudflare proved that you can hit record revenue while cutting 20% of headcount. Both things are happening.

But here's the problem: Salesforce and ServiceNow are not losing customers to Sierra. In fact, both companies have net revenue retention rates above 120%—meaning existing customers are *expanding* spend year-over-year. Salesforce had 12% of its 150,000-customer base on an Agentforce deal in Q4 2025, with only 6% on paid plans. That's extremely early. ServiceNow's NRR sat at 125% in Q3 2025. These are not signs of a market in collapse.

The real dynamic is different: **existing customers are staying, but their teams aren't growing as fast as they would have pre-AI, and vendors are struggling to expand per-seat revenue** because internal generative AI—not necessarily a third-party agent platform like Sierra—is handling work that used to require new hires.

**The margin compression part is even more interesting and underexplored:**

The Researcher correctly notes that SaaS vendors are facing margin headwinds. But the cause isn't seat loss—it's COGS (cost of goods sold). Serving AI features requires paying for:
- Token consumption on LLM APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic, etc.)
- Real-time inference infrastructure
- Eval engineering and safety validation (Q1 2026 earnings calls show vendors hiring 2–15 dedicated eval engineers per product team)

This is structural. A Salesforce sales rep doesn't get more expensive to serve in year two. An AI agent does. Every time a customer uses Agentforce, Salesforce pays Anthropic (their launch partner) or OpenAI for tokens. That's a variable cost of goods that didn't exist in the per-seat era. Margins compress from 80% to 67% not because customers are leaving, but because the cost to deliver the same value doubled.

**Now, the bigger strategic question the Researcher raises: will customers really shift away from per-seat pricing?**

The answer is *no, and yes, and it's more complicated than that.* Here's the truth:

Per-seat pricing is still alive. According to Profitwell's 2026 benchmark, 67% of SaaS companies still use tiered pricing models with per-seat components. It's not dead. But it's hybridizing. Salesforce alone has shipped three different pricing models for Agentforce in 18 months:
1. $2 per conversation (launched early 2025)
2. Flex Credits at $0.10 per action (rolled out May 2025)
3. AELA (unlimited agent use for a fixed fee on 2–3 year terms) (2026)

That's not a company that has figured out how to charge for AI. That's a company experimenting because the traditional per-seat model genuinely doesn't fit agent workloads.

**The hardest question: will Sierra actually threaten Salesforce's position?**

Here's where I diverge most sharply from the Researcher. Sierra raised $15.8 billion and is building an "independent" agent platform. But let's be precise about what they're building: a layer on top of Salesforce, HubSpot, Zendesk, Servicenow, and other SaaS backends.

The Researcher frames this as "Sierra captures value as customers shift from paying for human workers to paying for AI agents." But the data suggests something closer to: "Sierra is a specialized agent orchestration layer that *only works* if customers stay with their existing SaaS vendors and buy Sierra on top."

This matters because:
1. Salesforce has a 120%+ NRR and is adding Agentforce natively. If a customer is in Salesforce CRM, they'll try Agentforce first (same reason Microsoft bundles Copilot into 365). Network effects and integration gravity pull inward.
2. ServiceNow agents execute on the Now platform directly. Similar lock-in.
3. Sierra's most likely future is not "replace Salesforce" but "become a middleware that orchestrates agents across multiple SaaS platforms"—which makes it valuable but not a *replacement* for enterprise software, more of a *supplement*.

**The Law of Conservation of Attractive Profits applies here:**

Traditional SaaS margins were attractive (75–80%) because distribution was concentrated and switching costs were high. Now distribution is fracturing (new buyer workflows, agent-first architectures) and costs are shifting from vendor to infrastructure (tokens, eval engineering). But the profits don't disappear—they're being redistributed.

Salesforce and Servicenow *will* lose expansion dollars from seat compression. That's real. But they'll make it back (partially) through:
1. Agent add-on pricing (what Agentforce and Now AI agents already do)
2. Outcome pricing for certain workflows (Salesforce's AELA model is an early signal)
3. Capturing margin on the orchestration layer before it goes to third parties

Sierra will capture some of that margin. So will Anthropic, OpenAI, and other infrastructure providers (via tokens). But the idea that "Sierra is the platform that captures value as customers shift from per-seat to agentic pricing" assumes a much cleaner replacement than the data supports.

**The clearest insight the Researcher is right about:**

Headcount growth is decoupling from SaaS revenue growth. Cloudflare proved you can 20% your workforce and grow revenue. That is genuinely new and genuinely scary for vendors built on "more employees = more seats = more revenue." But the market response is already in motion: hybrid pricing, usage-based add-ons, and outcome models. These aren't signs of SaaS death; they're signs of SaaS evolution.

The incumbents who execute this transition fastest—Salesforce has three pricing models; ServiceNow is metering agent "assists"—will keep 80% of the market. New entrants like Sierra will capture 10–15% in specialized orchestration use cases. Margin will compress 10–15 points industry-wide and stay there as a floor. And the 30–40% seat reductions the Researcher cites will become the new baseline expectation.

**Key framework:** Law of Conservation of Attractive Profits (Charlie Munger / Mauboussin). Profits don't disappear from industries—they're redistributed. In this case, from SaaS vendors to LLM providers (via tokens), to infrastructure companies (eval engineers), and to specialized orchestration layers (Sierra-type platforms). The pie isn't shrinking; the slices are rearranging.

**Strongest counter-argument:** Salesforce and Servicenow's 120%+ NRR suggests they're *not* losing customers or market share—they're expanding existing customer value while managing seat compression. If the incumbents can successfully monetize agent layers within their platforms (which the data shows they're attempting), Sierra's "independent agent platform" thesis becomes a mid-market-only story, not a replacement thesis.
