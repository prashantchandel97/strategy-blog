## Monday | R | The End of the Seat: How AI Broke SaaS's Most Profitable Business Model

For 25 years, the per-seat SaaS model was the closest thing software had to a cash-printing machine. You sold licenses to Salesforce or HubSpot at $100–200 per user per month. Users multiplied, revenue compounded, valuations soared. The math was simple: more humans doing more work inside the software meant more seats to sell. It was so predictable that Wall Street priced SaaS companies at 10–15x revenue instead of the 2–3x multiple given to traditional software licensors.

That model is collapsing. Not failing—collapsing. And the breakdown is exposing something deeper about how business software actually generates value.

**The Trigger: Agents Don't Need Seats**

The inflection point was unmistakable. In February 2026, agentic AI products like Claude's Cowork and OpenAI's o1 demonstrated they could autonomously execute multi-step workflows inside SaaS applications. A company that once needed 100 customer service agents now needs 10 humans to supervise. The work is still happening inside the CRM or support ticket system—but now an AI agent is doing it. You don't buy seats for agents.

The market priced this in immediately. Salesforce dropped 40–45% YTD. Adobe fell 45%. ServiceNow declined 40%. The selloff wasn't a 10% valuation compression—it was existential repricing. Investors were saying: if you can't grow seats because agents do the work, your entire unit economics collapse.

Here's why the panic is rational. If a customer today licenses 100 seats of Salesforce at $165/month, that's ~$198,000 ARR. In two years, agentic AI means they only license 20 seats + agents. What do you charge for the agents? That's the question keeping SaaS CFOs awake.

**The Industry's Messy Answer: Hybrid Pricing**

Because per-seat is now clearly broken, companies are scrambling to invent alternatives. The data shows the chaos:

- **37% of SaaS companies now use a hybrid model** (Kyle Poyar's 2026 State of B2B Monetization survey, 230+ companies). Hybrid means: base subscription per seat + consumption metering on top for AI interactions.
- **Gartner forecasts 40% of enterprise SaaS will include outcome-based pricing by end of 2026**, up from just 15% in 2022. Three years ago, this was a fringe idea. Now it's mainstream.
- **Seat-based revenue share is collapsing**: Gartner projects it will fall from 21% of SaaS spend in 2024 to 15% by 2026—a 30% drop in just 24 months.

But "hybrid" is just the word companies use when they haven't figured out what pricing actually works.

Freshworks CEO Dennis Woodside was explicit: "AI pricing models will shift from pay-per-license to pay-per-interaction." His company now offers three tiers: per-agent pricing (old model), a software tier for AI co-pilots, and consumption-based pricing for AI interactions called "sessions." In Freshworks' first-quarter results, "sessions" as a unit of pricing grew faster than traditional seat growth. Translation: it's working, but it's a completely different unit economy.

HubSpot, which already dominates horizontal SaaS, moved to "core seats + AI consumption" hybrid. Salesforce is doing the same. These aren't experimental pilots—they're shipping this in production because they have to. If they don't de-commoditize seats, their growth crashes.

**Why This Matters More Than It Looks**

The SaaS pricing collapse is actually revealing two different futures:

1. **Measurement moats get harder, not easier.** When you charge per-seat, you measure one thing: users added. When you charge per-interaction or per-outcome, you measure dozens of things: what constitutes an "interaction"? How do you prevent customers from gaming the system? Does outcome pricing mean you share downside when the AI makes mistakes? Freshworks' Woodside said the metric they're driving is "development efficiency gains," not token consumption. That's measurement at a different level—it requires deeper integration into the customer's business, more instrumentation, more data. This actually makes the moat harder to copy, but it also makes it harder to execute. The companies that nail this first lock in asymmetric advantages for 2–3 years.

2. **Horizontal SaaS becomes vulnerable to vertical specialists again.** If Salesforce can't grow seats anymore, its main advantage—the same platform serving finance, ops, HR—becomes less defensible. A vertical CRM built for healthcare that charges outcome-based pricing for "faster claim denials" or "reduced customer churn" might outgrow the horizontal player on the same underlying technology. The category is re-segmenting.

The "SaaSpocalypse" (the term the market is actually using) is not a temporary selloff. It's a repricing of how much value per-seat software can ever generate. For 25 years, SaaS was the software business model that won. For the next 5 years, it's the one that has to be rebuilt.

## Monday | A | The Margin Trap: Why Agentic Pricing Isn't Actually Better Economics

The Researcher has nailed the diagnosis: per-seat SaaS is broken because agents don't need seats. That part is airtight. But there's a darker implication buried in the earnings reports and unit economics that the market is quietly repricing—one that suggests the cure might be worse than the disease.

Here's the unstated problem: AI agents don't just *eliminate the need for seats*. They *replace low-variable-cost software delivery with high-variable-cost compute delivery*. And that changes everything about who can actually be profitable.

**The Cost Structure Trap**

Traditional per-seat SaaS had beautiful unit economics. Once you shipped the software, the marginal cost of adding another user was essentially zero. You could charge $165/month for Salesforce and keep 80%+ gross margin because there was no extra compute, storage, or support per seat added. The software scaled horizontally. That zero-variable-cost structure is what made SaaS so valuable—and what enabled 10-15x revenue multiples.

But consumption-based pricing for AI—whether framed as "sessions," "tokens," "credits," or "agent hours"—creates a *direct, visible connection between what the customer pays and what the vendor actually costs*. Every token Claude processes costs OpenAI money. Every Anthropic API call costs Salesforce money. Freshworks said they're charging $100 for 1,000 sessions. But a fraction of that $100 goes straight to Anthropic or AWS for inference. That's not a margin compression issue. That's a structural margin ceiling.

Janelle Teng Wade at Next Big Teng spotted the exact problem: "The consumption layer has real variable costs, inference and hyperscaler compute, that the seat model never had. So even if they successfully pivot the pricing model, the margin profile downgrades from 'zero marginal cost' to 'sharing economics with Azure and Anthropic at two layers.'" That's not hyperbole. It's accounting.

Here's the math: if Freshworks charges $100 per 1,000 sessions, and a meaningful portion of that cost is OpenAI or Anthropic API fees (which are themselves consumption-based), Freshworks' gross margin on that $100 is not 80%. It's maybe 45-55% if they're efficient. The layer between Freshworks and the model provider is a fixed-cost moat built by the cloud/model providers, not Freshworks. And that moat eats SaaS margins.

The market is repricing SaaS not because the *revenue model* is changing. It's repricing because the *margin profile* is collapsing. And that collapses the valuation multiple.

**The Salesforce Counter-Narrative (And Why It's Fragile)**

There's one major counter-example: Salesforce. Their stock is down 30-40% YTD, but Agentforce ARR hit $800M in Q4 FY26, up 169% YoY. They've closed 29,000 agent deals. On the surface, this looks like the new model *is* working, and the market is just slow to recognize it.

But here's the critical detail the Researcher didn't flag: Salesforce is *embedding* the agents inside their platform. Their customers aren't paying Salesforce for compute—they're paying for the *integration* of agents with their CRM, the data orchestration, the business logic. Salesforce is leveraging their installed base and platform stickiness to command higher prices for *outcome orchestration*, not just raw agents. They're using Data 360 and industry-specific agents to create vertical distribution.

That's not the same business model as what ServiceNow or Freshworks is attempting. Salesforce is competing on bundled value, not on being cheaper than hiring humans. And it's working—at scale, for customers who already trust their ecosystem.

But that only works if you have Salesforce's installed base, brand, and customer lock-in. It doesn't work for the other 90% of SaaS companies that are trying to convert their seat-based products to consumption-based models.

**The Actual Repricing Story**

Here's what's actually happening: the market is separating into two tiers:

1. **Platform-scale consolidators** (Salesforce, HubSpot, ServiceNow, Adobe) that can afford to invest in AI, bundle it with existing products, and rely on customer switching costs. They'll survive the transition. HubSpot hit margin targets a year early in Q1 2026 while navigating the pricing reset. But notice they *raised guidance*—they have the scale to absorb the short-term friction.

2. **Everything else**: Companies trying to retrofit consumption pricing onto legacy seat-based architectures are caught. They can't match the infrastructure investments of the consolidators. They can't match the margins either, because they lack the installed base to justify the compute costs. Workday is down 26-38% YTD despite aggressively positioning AI. Monday.com is down 25%. These are real companies with real products, but they're structurally disadvantaged.

So when the Researcher says "horizontal SaaS becomes vulnerable to vertical specialists again," I have to push back. The vertical specialists are *also* vulnerable. The winners are consolidators with enough scale to absorb the margin compression and enough customer lock-in to command premium pricing for orchestrated agents.

**The Open Question: Can Outcome-Based Pricing Actually Work?**

The Researcher correctly identifies that outcome-based pricing *could* be a solution. If you price Salesforce on "faster deal cycles" or "lower customer churn," you align incentives and potentially unlock higher prices. But there's a game-theory problem: outcome pricing only works if the vendor has high confidence they can actually deliver the outcome consistently. That requires:

1. Deep integration into the customer's business (they need to see what's actually working)
2. Willingness to absorb downside when the agent fails or the business environment shifts
3. Ability to isolate the agent's contribution to the outcome from all other variables (this is almost impossible)

Gartner reports 43% of enterprise buyers want this, but wanting it ≠ vendors successfully delivering it at scale. The legal and operational complexity of outcome pricing at enterprise scale is massive. It also makes customer acquisition longer and riskier for the vendor.

What we're more likely to see is *permanent hybrid pricing*: a base subscription (lower than before) plus consumption meters (but with caps to manage vendor costs). That's what Freshworks, HubSpot, and Salesforce are all actually doing. It's not a breakthrough. It's a compromise that trades predictability for growth upside.

**The Real Question for Sunday**

The Researcher asked: "Does the shift to consumption/outcome pricing favor cloud infrastructure providers and hurt pure SaaS players?" Yes, absolutely. But the second part of that story is: it also favors SaaS consolidators with:
- $1B+ ARR (enough to justify ML/AI infrastructure investments)
- 10+ year customer relationships (lock-in for pricing power)
- Vertical industry distribution (to charge for outcomes)

Everyone else is caught in the margin trap, trying to retrofit consumption pricing onto a cost structure that was built for zero-variable-cost delivery.

The "SaaSpocalypse" isn't a temporary repricing. It's a permanent recalibration of who can be profitable in post-agent SaaS.

**Key framework:** Law of Conservation of Attractive Profits (Christensen). SaaS spent 25 years with a unique competitive advantage: zero marginal cost structure that allowed high multiples and rapid scaling. AI agents have shifted that advantage away from the SaaS vendor (who now has to pay for compute) and toward the infrastructure provider (who owns the chips and the models). The profit pool hasn't moved—it's migrated. Cloud and model infrastructure wins. Pure SaaS commoditizes unless you have consolidator scale.

**Strongest counter-argument:** Salesforce's Agentforce trajectory suggests the new model is working faster than the pessimists think. $800M ARR at 169% YoY growth means the company has already proven out the unit economics at scale. If Salesforce, HubSpot, and ServiceNow can all navigate the margin transition while maintaining or accelerating growth, the market's -40% repricing on *all* SaaS may be too severe. The counter-narrative is: the repricing is correct, but it's temporary. In 12-18 months, when consumption pricing stabilizes, the consolidators will re-rate upward.
